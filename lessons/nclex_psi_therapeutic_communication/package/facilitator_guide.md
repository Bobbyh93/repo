## Slide-by-slide

### S01 · Therapeutic Communication During Emotional Distress
*title · cross-lane · instructor-added · 60 s*  
**Objective:** Orient to the lesson's promise.  

**Script:**

Therapeutic Communication During Emotional Distress. The concept underneath it is therapeutic communication, and the module rates its safety risk high — its top rating, so this is one to be able to do under pressure rather than one to recognise on a test. Here is the whole lesson in one sentence. Use presence, observation, open invitations, reflection, and direct safety assessment without false reassurance or judgment. By the end you should be able to do three things: select therapeutic responses to distress; avoid common nontherapeutic communication barriers; and escalate statements that indicate possible self-harm. We get there in three moves, and then spend the second half putting all of them against patient cues, because knowing this and doing it at the bedside are not the same skill.

*opening_case · create space · source-aligned · 150 s*  
**Objective:** Enter the lesson through a single patient cue.  
**CJM:** recognize cues  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

We start with a cue rather than a definition, because that is the order the unit gives you: the patient arrives first and the explanation catches up later. Here it is. A patient says, "Nothing will ever get better." Decide now, before any of this is taught, what you would do and why that thing first. Write it somewhere you will still be able to read at the end, because this exact cue comes back as the first assessment item. Comparing the two answers is most of what the rest of this session is for.

### S06 · Create space
*concept_cards · create space · source-aligned · 150 s*  
**Objective:** Select therapeutic responses to distress.  
**CJM:** recognize cues, analyze cues  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

Create space. Use calm presence, observations, and open invitations that allow the patient to set the pace and describe their experience. This is the first of the three, and the two after it assume it. What you get wrong here does not stay here; it propagates quietly into every decision the rest of the lesson asks you to make. Do not move on until you can say this part back without the slide. The test is the objective: if you can select therapeutic responses to distress, this lane has done its job, and if you cannot, nothing later in the lesson will fix it — it will only hide it.

### S07 · Clarify
*concept_cards · clarify · source-aligned · 150 s*  
**Objective:** Avoid common nontherapeutic communication barriers.  
**CJM:** prioritize hypotheses, generate solutions  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

Clarify. Reflect or restate the patient's meaning and ask focused questions when safety or symptoms require clarification. This is the middle move, and it is where most of the disagreement in this lesson will sit: the point at which a defensible action and the priority action stop being the same answer. Do not move on until you can say this part back without the slide. The test is the objective: if you can avoid common nontherapeutic communication barriers, this lane has done its job, and if you cannot, nothing later in the lesson will fix it — it will only hide it.

### S08 · Assess safety
*concept_cards · assess safety · source-aligned · 150 s*  
**Objective:** Escalate statements that indicate possible self-harm.  
**CJM:** take action, evaluate outcomes  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

Assess safety. Statements suggesting hopelessness or self-harm require direct, nonjudgmental assessment and immediate protection according to risk. This is the last of the three, and it is the one most often left implicit -- the step learners can describe accurately and then do not actually perform when the first two have gone well. Say it out loud; that is how it survives contact with a busy shift. Do not move on until you can say this part back without the slide. The test is the objective: if you can escalate statements that indicate possible self-harm, this lane has done its job, and if you cannot, nothing later in the lesson will fix it — it will only hide it.

### S09 · Activity: match the cue to the move it calls for
*match_activity · apply · source-aligned · 170 s*  
**Objective:** Match each cue to the lesson move that answers it.  
**CJM:** recognize cues, analyze cues  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

Work in pairs, four minutes. Every cue on the left was written for one of the lesson moves on the right; your job is to say which, and to be able to defend one of the four out loud. Where the two of you match a cue differently, stop and stay there — you have found a cue whose lane is genuinely arguable, and those are worth more than the three you agreed on instantly. Bring one disagreement to the debrief.

**Activity:** Write the four matches and one sentence explaining one of them.

**Answer key:**
- A relates to: Create space
- B relates to: Clarify
- C relates to: Assess safety
- D relates to: Assess safety

### S10 · Debrief: the reasoning behind each action
*debrief · apply · source-aligned · 150 s*  
**Objective:** Explain why each keyed action follows from its cue.  
**CJM:** analyze cues  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

Read these four rationales as a set rather than one at a time. They are deliberately repetitive: the same habit of mind is showing up behind four different cues, and that repetition is the teaching point rather than an accident of writing. A learner who memorises four rules will meet a fifth cue and stall. A learner who can name the one habit will handle cues this lesson never showed them. Say the habit out loud in your own words before you leave this slide.

### S11 · Checkpoint
*checkpoint_mcq · clarify · source-aligned · 120 s*  
**Objective:** Choose the correct action for a single cue.  
**CJM:** analyze cues  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

One question, and it targets the middle of the lesson. The cue is: A grieving patient becomes silent and tearful. Read all four options and commit to one before the reveal, because the value of a checkpoint is in being wrong out loud rather than quietly. The reasoning you are being tested on is this: Therapeutic silence communicates presence without forcing conversation.

**Activity:** Choose the best response.

**Answer key:**
- C. Remain present and allow silence while offering the patient time to speak.

### S12 · Mini case
*mini_case · apply · source-aligned · 165 s*  
**Objective:** Respond to a cue using the lesson's frame.  
**CJM:** prioritize hypotheses, take action  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

A bedside moment, and this time you write rather than choose. The situation: A patient says, "Everyone is judging me." Write two sentences before the debrief: one naming what you assess, one naming what you do about it. Keep them in that order, because the order is the judgement. The keyed reasoning is: Reflection validates the expressed feeling and invites clarification.

**Activity:** Two sentences: what you assess, then what you do.

**Answer key:**
- Respond, "It sounds as though you feel judged; tell me more about that."
- Reflection validates the expressed feeling and invites clarification.

### S13 · Sort by urgency
*urgency_sort · apply · source-aligned · 165 s*  
**Objective:** Sort cues by whether they require immediate action.  
**CJM:** prioritize hypotheses  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

Two columns, and the sorting rule is the point of the slide. Put a cue on the left when delay changes the outcome for the patient, and on the right when the correct action is verification, planning or teaching that a short delay does not worsen. Justify one of your placements aloud. Learners who can sort these reliably are the ones who prioritise well under load.

**Activity:** Place each cue and justify one.

**Answer key:**
- Act now: the cues whose keyed action is immediate
- Act in sequence: the cues whose keyed action is verification or planning

### S15 · Capstone: choose the next safe action
*capstone_mcq · evaluate · source-aligned · 150 s*  
**Objective:** Select the priority action in the lesson's hardest cue.  
**CJM:** take action, evaluate outcomes  
**Sources:** SRC02  

**Script:**

The last question, and the hardest cue the topic carries: The nurse is tempted to say, "Everything will be fine." All four options are things a nurse might plausibly do, which is what makes it a capstone rather than a checkpoint. Choose the priority action, not merely a defensible one. The reasoning that separates them: False reassurance can close communication and minimize distress.

**Activity:** Choose the priority action.

**Answer key:**
- B. Acknowledge uncertainty and explore the patient's specific concern instead.
